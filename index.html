About Me

I am a Mechanical Engineering BS/MS student at WPI with a minor in music, expecting to graduate in May 2027. My focus is on structural dynamics, vibration testing, and signal processing. My thesis, advised by Prof. Zhu Mao, centers on Statistical Energy Analysis (SEA) and Shock Response Spectrum (SRS) model validation. I am also helping to develop a graduate-level course on signal processing, handling the workflow from the experimental setups to the course materials.

This past summer, I was a LADSS Fellow at Los Alamos National Laboratory, where I designed experimental test methodology end to end for soft-material dynamic characterization: instrumentation, compression testing, impact excitation, full-field Digital Image Correlation (DIC), and post-processing to inform finite-element validation on Abaqus.

Previously, I was an undergraduate research assistant on a PhD thesis project studying vibration damping in polymer foams, funded through Saint-Gobain. Under Prof. Mao's Laboratory of Intelligent Systems and Structural Dynamics (LISSD) at WPI, I led guitar modal and acoustic analysis using impact hammers, modal shakers, accelerometers, and a Laser Doppler Vibrometer (LDV) to understand how build quality, body deformities, material, and string price point affect acoustic quality.

Aside from my work in experimental modal analysis and vibro-acoustic testing, I lead the Air Piano project, a gesture-controlled MIDI instrument that started off as a passion project then became my senior capstone project.

Tools

Traditional quasi-static compression testing can't fully capture how materials behave under dynamic, impact-driven loading. My work explored whether stereo Digital Image Correlation (DIC), which captures full-field strain data simultaneously across a specimen, could serve as a better-suited alternative for characterizing nonlinear foam behavior under impact excitation.

Read more

I used stereo DIC (Vic-3D, Photron PFV4) for full-field strain measurement under impact loading, diagnosing and resolving camera-sync calibration drift to bring residual scores back under target threshold. I also designed an end-to-end DAQ and hardware trigger chain (NI PXIe, custom Python/NI-DAQmx scripts) to synchronously acquire load-cell and accelerometer data and trigger the high-speed DIC cameras on impact, eliminating analog trigger jitter. The resulting datasets were processed into stress-strain curves with compliance correction and used to calibrate a HYPERFOAM material model in Abaqus, validating test-simulation correlation for finite-element modeling.

This fellowship was led by Shannon Danforth and Samantha Ceballes, and mentored by Jim DeClerck, Kennan Hodovic, Christopher Teti, Peter Beck, and Samuel Hinerman.

Guitar Acoustics & Modal Analysis

Role
Researcher
Advisor
Prof. Zhu Mao
Timeline

Instruments
Crystal Instruments DAQ, LDV, MATLAB

This project investigates whether guitar price and build quality measurably affect acoustic performance, or whether perceived differences in tone owe more to construction and materials than to cost. Rather than relying on how the instruments sounded by ear, we approached the question from a dynamic standpoint, a more objective, measurable basis for comparison. I tested three guitars and six strings across a range of price points, build quality, and visible deformities, comparing frequency response and harmonic content to see what the data actually showed.

Read more

I led the full experimental workflow for guitar modal and acoustic analysis under Prof. Zhu Mao at WPI: configured impact hammer, modal shaker, accelerometers, and a Laser Doppler Vibrometer to measure structural vibrational response across body shapes, bracing, and tonewoods.

To understand how build quality affects acoustic quality, I bought guitars and strings at different price points, including instruments with cracks and deformities, and compared their frequency response functions and harmonic profiles. I extracted natural frequencies, damping ratios, and mode shapes with a Crystal Instruments DAQ and MATLAB (pwelch), the same vibro-acoustic techniques used in speaker, enclosure, and instrument design.

The results showed that guitar body quality, size, material, and construction had a far greater influence on acoustic performance than string price. Differences between strings of the same material were subtle and often negligible.

# CircuitPython: debounced button + smoothed pot MIDI firmwareimport time import board import digitalio import analogio import usb_midi import adafruit_midi from adafruit_midi.note_on import NoteOn from adafruit_midi.note_off import NoteOff from adafruit_midi.control_change import ControlChange BUTTON_PINS = [ board.GP2, board.GP3, board.GP4, board.GP5, board.GP6, board.GP7, board.GP8, board.GP9, board.GP10 ] PAD_NOTES = [ 45, 46, 47, 41, 42, 43, 37, 38, 39 ] NOTE_VELOCITY = 127 DEBOUNCE_S = 0.02 # 20 ms POT1 = analogio.AnalogIn(board.GP26) POT2 = analogio.AnalogIn(board.GP27) POT1_CC = 74 POT2_CC = 12 SMOOTH = 0.10 # lower = less lag DEADBAND = 6 # higher = no CC spam POT_DELAY = 0.01 # slower polling def adc_to_cc(v): return (v * 127) // 65535 midi = adafruit_midi.MIDI( midi_out=usb_midi.ports[1], out_channel=0 ) buttons = [] last_raw = [] stable = [] last_flip = [] t0 = time.monotonic() for pin in BUTTON_PINS: b = digitalio.DigitalInOut(pin) b.switch_to_input(pull=digitalio.Pull.UP) buttons.append(b) v = b.value last_raw.append(v) stable.append(v) last_flip.append(t0) p1 = adc_to_cc(POT1.value) p2 = adc_to_cc(POT2.value) last_p1 = p1 last_p2 = p2 while True: now = time.monotonic() # ---- Buttons (debounced) ---- for i, b in enumerate(buttons): raw = b.value if raw != last_raw[i]: last_raw[i] = raw last_flip[i] = now if (now - last_flip[i]) >= DEBOUNCE_S and raw != stable[i]: stable[i] = raw note = PAD_NOTES[i] if not stable[i]: # pressed midi.send(NoteOn(note, NOTE_VELOCITY)) else: # released midi.send(NoteOff(note, 0)) r1 = adc_to_cc(POT1.value) r2 = adc_to_cc(POT2.value) p1 = int((1 - SMOOTH) * p1 + SMOOTH * r1) p2 = int((1 - SMOOTH) * p2 + SMOOTH * r2) if abs(p1 - last_p1) >= DEADBAND: midi.send(ControlChange(POT1_CC, p1)) last_p1 = p1 if abs(p2 - last_p2) >= DEADBAND: midi.send(ControlChange(POT2_CC, p2)) last_p2 = p2 time.sleep(POT_DELAY)

Rice Husk Prescription Bottles

Role
R&D Researcher
Team
7 Team Members, 2 Advisors
Timeline
Aug 2023 – Dec 2023
Tools
SolidWorks, FDM 3D Printing

Nearly 165 million plastic prescription bottles enter landfills each year in the United States. Our team set out to find a biodegradable alternative that addresses the same functional requirements as standard prescription bottles, UV resistance, durability, and contamination resistance, while eliminating the plastic waste problem. "The Rice Remedy" was selected as the seminar's 2023 class winner for innovation in sustainable manufacturing, with material and medical applications extending well beyond pill bottles.

Read more

Plastic pill bottles are difficult to recycle: they're made primarily from #5 polypropylene, which isn't universally accepted in curbside recycling programs, and their opaque orange coloring causes many facilities to reject them outright. We explored a range of biodegradable composite materials and found that rice husks, a byproduct of one of the world's largest agricultural industries, met every requirement we were targeting. After testing several binding ingredients and techniques to achieve a consistent, homogeneous texture, we identified carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) powder as the safest option for consumer use while still binding the material effectively. We designed and fabricated molds in SolidWorks, using FDM 3D printing for casting to improve production efficiency and structural integrity.

The result: a viable, biodegradable substitute for polypropylene prescription bottles, with potential applications extending into other areas of the medical field.

This project was completed with a team of 7 as part of the Great Problems Seminar, advised by Prof. Svetlana Nikitina and Srivatsan Mukunthraj.
